*On publishing the Korean edition of* Out of the Shadows

**“Just something casual.” “I don’t date.”**

I wonder what people mean when they say this. Are they telling me what kind of encounter they want right now, or asking me to come no closer? Of course, some people prefer casual encounters. Sex can be worthwhile in its own right. Being clear about your desires and boundaries can be a way of respecting each other.

The same words can come from very different places. For one person, a casual encounter is exactly what they want. For another, it’s the only kind of encounter in which they can relax. Some people prefer to live alone. Others want to share their lives with someone and have come to believe that such a life is out of reach. From the outside, it can be hard to tell the difference.

That is where the question I found in Walt Odets’s *Out of the Shadows* begins. Why do so many gay men long for love and come to fear intimacy? Why might someone who wants to get close find himself caught in a pattern of hurt and withdrawal? What conditions give rise to those feelings?

I wanted the title of the Korean edition to carry that question. The original title evokes people emerging from shame and silence as they come to accept their lives and identities. I wondered how that idea might reach Korean readers. If hiding who you are also shapes how you love, what does stepping out of the shadows mean for a relationship? How much time can pass between knowing you’re gay and living openly enough to love someone as a gay man?

The Korean title asks, *Why Can’t We Bear Love?* I chose it to ask about the lives lived in that interval.

A man might meet other men for sex and still have no one he can tell about loving a man. He might have a partner he can’t introduce to his family or mention in passing when coworkers ask what he did over the weekend. In ordinary conversation, he has to speak as if the person he cares for doesn’t exist. As the relationship deepens, there is more he wants to share and more he has to hide.

In those circumstances, sharing a life can feel harder than taking your clothes off. Intimacy brings questions you could leave aside when you met at an agreed time and went your separate ways. Where can we be together comfortably? How do we introduce each other, and to whom? Who gets to stay by the bedside when one of us is ill? Can we make plans for a life together?

Wanting to love someone won’t settle those questions. Letting someone into your life means looking again at the life you’ve held together by keeping part of yourself hidden. You can want to open up and fear the changes that opening up might bring. This is how the paradox of meeting someone to get close and feeling more anxious as you grow closer takes shape in everyday life.

For some people, “I only want something casual” is a statement of what they want. For others, the same sentence gives them a way to leave a further desire unspoken. They may want to stay a little longer, meet on a day when they don’t have sex, or call when something goes wrong. Even then, they draw their feelings back inside the boundary they set at the start. An encounter that began as a way to feel less lonely becomes another place to conceal loneliness.

Reading Odets made me want to pay attention to what people have been taught to do with their feelings and desires. When keeping your feelings secret helped you stay safe, being open can take a long time to feel easy. If you had to hide your attraction to men and leave it out even when you talked about your future, the habit of hiding may persist after you fall in love. You can want a relationship and still guard against letting it take up too much room in your life. A way of protecting yourself can make it harder to reach someone you want to be close to.

That is part of what I mean by finding love hard to bear. The way the world looks at you can become part of how you see yourself. Discrimination can shape what you think you’re allowed to want, how much you dare to hope for, and what kind of life you assume you’ll have to give up. After years of being looked at with contempt, someone may doubt another person’s affection. After being taught to feel ashamed of desire, it can be difficult to make a life and a relationship out of that desire.

That’s why the word *why* mattered to me. I wanted to understand how someone came to fear intimacy, what comes back when they begin to love, and how trusting someone became so difficult.

This is also why the history of HIV/AIDS matters to the book. For people who repeatedly lost friends and loved ones, the losses left a mark on everyday life, on relationships, and on their sense of a future. Another person could die before there was time to grieve, and much of that grief had to be carried amid stigma and silence.

A new love can bring those years back into view. The dearer someone becomes, the more frightening it may be to lose them. Imagining a future together can be a joy and a reminder of futures that disappeared. That fear can be hard to understand from the present relationship alone.

Gay men’s lives vary across generations and circumstances, and even the same experience can leave people with different feelings. Odets’s book keeps bringing me back to the history a person has lived before I make sense of what I see them doing in a relationship. The fear of love has a history, too.

Understanding that history opens another question, which I also wanted the Korean subtitle to hold. What kinds of relationships can we create from here? How do we write lives of our own when the forms we’ve inherited leave little room for our desires and experiences?

For me, rewriting begins with examining what we’ve learned about a successful relationship, how a man should behave, and what an ordinary life is supposed to look like. If living by those standards has required you to erase part of what you feel, you can begin to imagine other ways to live.

Odets shows gay men already doing this. They care for each other, live through losses together, and build bonds that the words *lover*, *friend*, and *family* can only partly describe. Those bonds take shape in who stays when someone is ill, who can hear another person’s fears, and who gets invited into a conversation about the future.

Reading their stories, I thought that our ability to bear love might also grow through relationships. We can learn what it feels like to speak a wish without being mocked, to show vulnerability and still be respected, and to talk through different needs together. The strength once spent on hiding can go into making a life of our own.

A casual encounter can be exactly what you want, and a deeper relationship can be something you want without shame. The question is whether you can get to know what you want and choose it before fear has drawn the limits of the life you think you can have.

Through the Korean title, these are the questions I wanted to ask readers. What have we been taught to fear? How does that fear constrain the way we love today? Once we understand the conditions that shaped it, what kinds of lives and relationships can we create?

“Just something casual. I don’t date.”

I hope the person who says this can know what they want and feel free to say it. If they want to live differently one day, I hope they can keep that possibility open, too. In publishing this book, I wanted to think together with readers about a love that can widen, little by little, the possibilities of a life.
